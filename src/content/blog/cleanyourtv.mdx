### Overview

This project is my own build of cleanyourtv, an open source project for cleaning up Android TVs that replaces the
stock home screen with a lightweight launcher. I ran it on my TCL 43S450G and then made it mine: a Continue watching
row and five home screens I designed myself (Feature, Minutes, Horizon, Centre Line and Lamp), switched with a Themes
button right on the TV. I built all of it with Claude Code in a few days.

### Goals of the Project

- Get rid of the ads and the slowness. The stock Google TV home screen was full of ads, and the TV felt slow.
- Make it my own. Cleaning up the TV was the starting point, but I knew I could take the launcher further and make
  something I liked better.
- Come up with my own designs. A home screen is something you look at every day, and I wanted it to look the way I
  wanted, not the way a TV maker decided.
- Own my software. Every change is reversible, nothing is rooted or uninstalled, and the code is mine to change.
- See how far AI coding agents have come, by building a real Android project on real hardware with one.

### Technologies Used

- **Android TV:** The platform the launcher runs on, here Google TV 11 on a TCL set with 2 GB of RAM.
- **Java:** The language of the cleanyourtv launcher, and of everything I added to it.
- **ADB:** How I talked to the TV: disabling packages, installing builds, pressing remote buttons and taking
  screenshots from my computer.
- **Claude Code:** The AI agent that wrote the code, from my mock-ups and decisions.

### Backstory

cleanyourtv was popular online, and I thought, why not give it a shot? My TV had the usual Google TV problems: ads
all over the home screen and a general feeling of slowness. The project's approach was simple: back everything up,
disable the bloat (never uninstall it), and replace the home screen with a small, fast launcher. I ended up
disabling 27 packages, including the stock launcher, the voice assistant and Chromecast, and the TV immediately felt
better.

But I also knew going in that I could make it my own, and better. The stock launcher is clean, but it's someone
else's design. So I started with the thing I missed most: a Continue watching row, so the shows I'm halfway through
in Jellyfin and Netflix are right there on Home.

Then I got into the fun part. I designed home screens as HTML mock-ups on my laptop, tried a bunch of ideas, and
kept five:

- **Feature:** a big cinema banner of whatever's focused, with progress shown as a strip of film.
- **Minutes:** a timeline of how much time is left in each show, shortest first, for "what can I finish before
  bed?"
- **Horizon:** a sky that follows the time of day, from dawn to night.
- **Centre Line:** calm and centred, with stars coming out in the evening.
- **Lamp:** a dim, warm room where only the focused item is lit, like a lamp following you around.

From there I built them one at a time with Claude Code. The loop was simple: build a step, install it on the TV,
look at it, press the real remote, decide what to change. Some of my favourite decisions came from that loop. Lamp's
first dim was too dark, so it got lighter. Centre Line had a little focus dot that looked fine in the mock-up but
not on the TV, so it went. I tried a sky setting for Horizon and decided the sky should simply follow the clock.

Honestly, there were no big surprises. It was a simple few-day project. That's what struck me most: how easy it is
now to build something like this with an AI agent, and how good the models have become. I made the designs and the
decisions, and Claude turned them into working Java on a real TV.

### What I Learned

- **Seeing it on the TV is what matters.** A mock-up on a laptop and a screen across the room aren't the same thing.
  Several of my decisions only came after seeing a theme on the TV and using it with the real remote.
- **Android TV apps share less than you'd think.** On my TV only Jellyfin and Netflix publish anything to Watch Next,
  the list behind Continue watching. Prime Video, YouTube and Disney+ publish nothing.
- **Fonts are heavy.** The original project keeps its launcher under 576 KB because it targets TVs with 1 GB of RAM.
  My build is 724 KB, and almost all of the difference is the fonts I gave three of the themes, even cut down. I
  kept them on purpose: on a 2 GB TV the type is worth more than the kilobytes, and that's exactly the kind of call
  owning your own fork lets you make.
- **Keep network access tight.** Netflix's posters are web addresses, so showing them meant giving the launcher
  internet access. I limited it to Netflix's image server only, with every picture cached on the TV.
- **TVs are bad at recording themselves.** I wanted demo videos for the README, but this TV's screen recorder only
  catches about one frame per key press, so the README has screenshots instead.

### Ending Up in the Showcase

Because my fork adds permissions and goes over the size limit, it was never meant to go back into the original
project as a pull request. The project has a Showcase page for exactly that: projects built on top of it that live in
their own repositories. I wrote a proper README for the fork with screenshots of all five themes, opened a pull
request adding it to the Showcase, and it was merged. Seeing my fork listed in the original repo was a nice way to
finish a project that started with "why not give it a shot?"
